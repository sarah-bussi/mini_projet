Collaborative project · 2026

BRA(S)VO

Connected orthosis · Sensors · BLE

Problem addressed

Hand rehabilitation can feel repetitive, demotivating and difficult to continue at home. Patients receive little immediate feedback about their progress, while therapists need objective measurements to adapt exercises.

Designed solution

BRA(S)VO combines an instrumented orthosis, a Flutter application and five therapeutic mini-games. The prototype targets people with impaired hand mobility, particularly following a stroke or with Dupuytren’s disease, together with the occupational and physical therapists supporting them.

My contribution

  • Development lead within a multidisciplinary five-person team.
  • Co-development of the Flutter application with Hocine.
  • Integration of sensor data into the games with Marie-Ly.
  • Contribution to functional decisions, user testing, feedback analysis and shared deliverables.

Functional architecture

  1. Orthosis: five flex sensors measure finger range, five FSR sensors measure pressure and an IMU tracks hand orientation and movement.
  2. Embedded electronics: an ESP32 collects measurements and sends them over Bluetooth Low Energy through CircuitPython firmware.
  3. Application: Flutter rebuilds occasionally fragmented BLE frames, extracts values, normalises them and applies the thresholds used by the games.
  4. Data: an eight-table local SQLite database stores accounts, sessions, scores, sensor readings, appointments and messages offline.

Two complementary journeys

Patient mode

  • Daily session and mini-games
  • Results, badges and progress
  • History and regularity
  • Messages and BLE connection

Therapist mode

  • Patient dashboard
  • Session planning
  • Game selection and adaptation
  • Remote monitoring and messaging

Five developed mini-games

  • Catch the bubblesFSR pressure, precision, reaction time and progressive difficulty.
  • Digital pianoIndependent flexion of five fingers, rhythm and coordination.
  • Magic gardenA gesture sequence combining flexion, pressure, pinching and inclination.
  • Fill the bottleSimultaneously maintain target pressure and a flexion threshold.
  • Balance the ballIMU-based wrist orientation with automatically adapted difficulty.

User testing and limitations

A single-session test involved ten M2 students without motor impairments. The prototype was generally perceived as lightweight, playful and engaging, and the approximate 60-second duration per level was considered appropriate. Independent donning, sensor placement and long-term comfort still require improvement.

The tests assessed an initial experience rather than clinical effectiveness. Calibration, solder reliability, BLE stability and incomplete accessibility features still limited full use of the system: BRA(S)VO remains a university prototype, not a validated medical device.

ERUA · Sofia · 24 October 2025

Digital Accessibility Workshop

Event context

The workshop was hosted by New Bulgarian University (NBU) in Sofia and organised by its Department of Cognitive Science and Psychology. Five students from Université Paris 8 contributed to the programme for an audience from NBU and the ERUA consortium.

My contribution

My presentation, Cognitive and Psychological Approach to Digital Accessibility, focused specifically on the cognitive, psychological and emotional dimensions of using digital interfaces.

  1. Defining cognitive accessibility: considering dyslexia, ADHD, autism, brain injury and intellectual disability, together with everyday situations involving fatigue, stress or distraction.
  2. Understanding cognitive diversity and mental workload: connecting differences in attention, memory, problem-solving and learning pace with the limits of working memory.
  3. Applying psychological principles: using visual organisation, recognition rather than recall, motivating feedback and error tolerance to support users.
  4. Turning theory into design strategies: clear and Easy-to-Read language, consistent navigation, structured headings, explicit actions, motion control, multimodal communication, AAC and distraction-free reading modes.
  5. Highlighting emotional impact: showing how accessible interfaces can support autonomy, competence and dignity, while confusing experiences can cause stress, avoidance and loss of confidence.

Knowledge-sharing format

An eight-part presentation delivered in English, combining design examples, a forward-looking summary and collective discussion with an international academic audience.

Game accessibility · 2025

SPY

Context

SPY is an open-source serious game developed by the Mocah team at LIP6 to introduce primary-school learners to computational thinking and programming. It includes 6 scenarios, 59 levels, a level editor and a scenario editor.

Audit approach

As part of a three-student project, the interface was reviewed using the Game Accessibility Guidelines. The assessment covered six categories of needs — motor, cognitive, visual, hearing, speech and general — across the basic, intermediate and advanced recommendation levels.

Findings and design responses

  1. Moving programming blocks: the available interactions were not sufficiently explicit. We proposed visual and written guidance covering alternatives including keyboard, mouse, joystick and eye-control input.
  2. Interaction precision: selecting and moving elements could require fine motor control. We proposed adjustable mouse and touchpad sensitivity.
  3. Interface element size: some components were small and difficult to identify or manipulate. We proposed a global interface-size setting.
  4. Execution speed: program actions could be difficult to follow. We proposed adjustable slow playback to support understanding of the relationship between an action and its outcome.
  5. Contrast and colour: one audited colour combination had a 2.98:1 contrast ratio. We proposed alternative themes with stronger contrast and a calmer palette.

Selected mock-ups

Three representative proposals show how the recommendations were translated into concrete interface solutions.

Project outcome

The team produced an assessment matrix, a requirements report and six solution mock-ups. The improvements were designed and prototyped but were not implemented in the game’s source code.

Connected device · stress & biofeedback · 2026

SmartCare Watch

Problem explored

SmartCare Watch is a two-student university project exploring a cardiorespiratory wristband that could identify stress states and provide immediate visual and haptic biofeedback. Its primary audience is people prone to stress or anxiety, particularly in study and workplace contexts.

My contribution

I designed the interface mock-ups and developed the Android application. I also worked on the BLE circuits, the simulation screen, the integration of the M5StickC Plus with the BPM sensor and Grove vibration motor, and then contributed to functional testing and technical adjustments.

Prototyped architecture

  1. Acquisition: reading an I²C BPM sensor on an M5StickC Plus and preparing an additional respiratory measurement.
  2. Communication: a BLE service able to notify BPM values to Android and receive a vibration command.
  3. Interpretation: a rule-based agent classifying values as relaxed, normal or stressed using cardiorespiratory thresholds.
  4. Biofeedback: the Flufy avatar, messages, exercise recommendations, guided breathing and discreet vibration.

What works and what remains simulated

Functional prototypes

  • BPM sensor detection and value reading;
  • haptic motor activation;
  • Bangle.js demonstrator with heart rate, 4–4–6–2 breathing and vibration cues;
  • Android journey: dashboard, history, emotion journal, recommendations and exercises.

Prototype limitations

  • partly simulated physiological data within the application;
  • real-time Android-to-hardware synchronisation not completed;
  • rule-based agent rather than a trained machine-learning model;
  • real-world testing limited to simulations or one person.

Outcome

The project demonstrates the feasibility of its essential components and provides a coherent foundation for future end-to-end integration. It remains an exploratory university prototype without clinical validation or medical-device status.

Accessible serious game · HCI · 2025

Braille-JP

Problem addressed

Braille-JP is a two-person project developed with Emilien Monet and supported by the ProgAccess association. It addresses the shortage of playful, accessible resources through which visually impaired people can learn braille in a foreign language, with an initial version focused on Japanese.

Audiences and journeys

Player journey

  • discover the alphabet, numbers and punctuation;
  • complete memorisation, reading and writing exercises;
  • track progress and personalise the experience;
  • use a tutorial, help and accessibility settings.

Contributor journey

  • add a language without needing to program;
  • provide characters, transcriptions and pronunciations;
  • supply audio files and language-specific rules;
  • submit content to the community for validation.

Design approach

  1. User-centred design: player and contributor personas, detailed scenarios and evaluation criteria.
  2. Association collaboration: progress reviews with ProgAccess approximately every two weeks.
  3. Initial feedback: a version shared with around ten visually impaired users, mainly association members.
  4. Ergonomic references: Bastien & Scapin criteria and Nielsen heuristics for guidance, workload, feedback and error prevention.

Planned accessibility

The prototype targets a desktop application usable with JAWS, NVDA or VoiceOver and a braille display. Navigation is designed for the keyboard, with contextual help through F1, volume adjustment through F2 and F3, voice messages, audio feedback and explicit shortcuts.

Status and limitations

The report documents an interactive mock-up and an initial beta version. Feedback was open and exploratory; the available time did not allow several testing cycles or a longitudinal evaluation of learning outcomes.

Outcome

Braille-JP establishes the foundations of an accessible, modular serious game open to community contribution. The project validates the usage direction and navigation architecture but should not be presented as a completed product.
